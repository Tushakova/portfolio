The definitions, without the ambiguity

α: A false alarm. Technically P(reject H₀ | H₀ true).

Nominal confidence: The intended long-run coverage of an interval procedure under its assumptions. It is not the probability that this observed interval contains a fixed true effect.

β: Missing the specified real effect. Technically P(fail to reject H₀ | specified H₁).

Power: Detecting the specified real effect. Technically P(reject H₀ | specified H₁). There is no single power value for every possible true effect.

Read the graph & understand the approximation

The curves show possible estimates of treatment minus control under each assumed truth, not individual users. The centre of H₁ is an assumption, not an observed result. Areas belong to their own curves, not their overlap.

We use a normal approximation with allocation-weighted pooled variance at the null boundary and separate arm variances under the specified alternative. Real tests estimate the pooled rate from each sample; theoretical power is an approximation. Sparse expected counts make it less reliable. One-sided testing is not equivalence or non-inferiority testing.

See the formulas behind this chart

D = treatment − control. Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function; z is its quantile.

p_pool = (n₀p₀ + n₁p₁) / (n₀ + n₁)
SE₀ = √[p_pool(1−p_pool)(1/n₀ + 1/n₁)]
SE₁ = √[p₀(1−p₀)/n₀ + p₁(1−p₁)/n₁]
c = z(1−α/2) × SE₀
Reject H₀ when D̂ < −c or D̂ > c
Power = Φ((−c−D)/SE₁) + 1−Φ((c−D)/SE₁)
β = 1−Power

Planning finds the smallest integer control n meeting desired power, with treatment n rounded up to the chosen allocation. At extreme settings the minimum of two users may already exceed the target. The normal approximation is unreliable at very small counts.

The estimate and its uncertainty

95% CONFIDENCE

Statistical significance does not establish commercial value. Consider effect size, uncertainty, implementation cost and guardrail metrics.

Which test and interval are used?

The Newcombe interval combines Wilson score intervals and avoids the collapsed uncertainty of a simple Wald interval at zero conversions. This interval and the test are not exact inverses: borderline decisions may differ.

A p-value is the probability, under the null model, of a test statistic at least as extreme as observed. It is not the probability that the null is true. Nominal confidence refers to the long-run coverage of a procedure, not a posterior probability for this interval.

More users → less variable averages. With independent observations and finite variance, the standardised sampling distribution approaches normality. Heavy tails can make that convergence slow.
Two-proportion z-test

Simple: Compare conversion rates.

Technical: A large-sample normal test of the difference between independent Bernoulli proportions, with pooled variance under the null of equal rates.

Welch’s t-test

Simple: Compare means without assuming equal variability.

Technical: A two-sample mean test using separate variance estimates and Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. Very heavy tails may need much larger samples; it is not an automatic cure for outliers.

Normal and Student’s t distributions

Simple: Symmetric bell-shaped distributions; t has heavier tails.

Technical: Student’s t represents a standard normal divided by the square root of an independent chi-square variable divided by its degrees of freedom. It reflects uncertainty from estimated variance and approaches normality as degrees of freedom increase.

The Central Limit Theorem & this simulation

Simple: Averages can become approximately normal even when individual values are not.

Technical: For independent, identically distributed observations with finite variance, the centred, scaled sample mean converges in distribution to a standard normal. Individual data do not become normal.

Our finite-variance mixture is 80% zeros; 19% lognormal with log-mean 3.4 and log-SD 0.65; 1% lognormal with log-mean 6 and log-SD 0.65. Samples are independent. The fixed seed makes comparisons reproducible.

Assumptions & responsible interpretation

Independent users, fixed horizon, a pre-specified two-sided or directional one-sided test, no repeated-peeking or multiple-testing correction. No clustering, attrition or delayed-outcome model. Traffic duration is a minimum: allow full business cycles and outcome maturation. An insignificant result does not prove equivalence. Two-sided tests can reject in either direction; one-sided tests only reject in the selected direction. Choose before seeing results; significance alone does not imply business value.

Browser calculations are independently checked against SciPy and statsmodels; a separate older Python/Streamlit edition may not include the new directional modes. Normal CDF approximations can differ at the last decimal place. Conversion simulation uses independent binomial draws; sparse conversion tables use Fisher’s exact test. Mean simulation uses exact summaries from explicitly normal populations and Welch analysis; it is not a realistic revenue generator. Mean planning treats supplied SDs as fixed and uses normal power, so achieved Welch rejection rates can differ. Heavy tails, small samples and uncertain historical SDs require additional assessment.
